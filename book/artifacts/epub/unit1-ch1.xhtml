The Power of Communication

Imagine a day without communication. You cannot ask for directions, explain an idea, tell someone how you feel, or understand what they need. Buying food, attending a class, making a friend, or working with colleagues would be difficult. Communication is not just a subject to study. It is part of how we take part in everyday life.

Communication involves more than speaking well. It includes listening carefully, asking meaningful questions, understanding body language, writing clearly, and responding appropriately. The purpose is not simply to finish what you want to say. It is to exchange meaning. That makes communication a two-way process: you need to express your own ideas and make room for someone else’s.

It is tempting to wait until your English feels perfect before joining a conversation. But waiting also means missing opportunities to practise. Start with the language you have. Notice when your listener understands you and when you need to explain something differently. Ask for feedback, then try again. A mistake gives you something specific to work on; it does not mean the conversation has failed.

Consider two students, Ali and Sara. Ali knows thousands of English words but rarely speaks because he is afraid of making mistakes. Sara knows fewer words, but she joins discussions, asks questions, and uses feedback to improve. In this example, Sara becomes a more confident speaker through regular practice. Ali improves more slowly because he avoids communication. The contrast is not about who is more intelligent. It is about how each student uses opportunities to speak.

Listening deserves the same attention as speaking. Good listeners follow the speaker’s ideas and consider their feelings and intentions. They can ask follow-up questions or briefly restate the main point to check their understanding. Eye contact and body language vary across people and cultures; attention does not have to look the same for everyone. What matters is making an effort to understand, rather than simply waiting for your turn.

Communication also helps people solve problems and share knowledge. Families use it to make decisions; colleagues use it to agree on action. Teachers explain lessons, doctors ask about patients’ needs, and scientists explain their research. Each situation calls for different language, but each depends on making meaning clear.

Like learning to drive, play football, or use a computer, learning to communicate takes practice. Try a clearer example, a useful question, or a revised explanation. Notice what helps your listener, and choose what to work on next.

When you hesitate to speak English, start with the point you want to make. Use the words you know, listen carefully, and ask for clarification when you need it. You can work on accuracy while taking part. Let each conversation guide what you practise next.

Speaking through Reading

Activity 1: One-Minute Summary

Work with a partner.

Student A: Give a one-minute summary in your own words. Include the main idea and one supporting example.

Student B: Listen without interrupting. Ask two questions that help you understand the summary better.

Switch roles. After both summaries, choose one explanation to make clearer and say it again.

Activity 2: Find the Evidence

Work in small groups.

Find three ideas in the reading that support the statement below. Note where each idea appears and explain how it supports the statement.

Communication is a skill that anyone can improve.

Share your evidence with the class. Distinguish what the reading says from an example of your own.
