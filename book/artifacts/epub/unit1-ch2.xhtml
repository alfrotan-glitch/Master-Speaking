Listening Strategy

First Listening

Listen to the whole story without reading the script. What is the main problem or decision? Give a one-sentence answer.

Second Listening

Read the detail questions, listen again, and take short notes in a notebook under the two headings below. Ask to hear a part again if you need it.

Main PointDetails I Heard

Listening Comprehension

Answer in short phrases. Use details you heard, not guesses about what usually happens.

  1. What caused the uncertainty?
  2. Which room did the club use?
  3. Why was the usual room unavailable?
  4. How did the speaker check the meeting plan?
  5. What responsibility did the speaker take on?

Compare and Clarify

Compare answers with a partner. Explain which detail supports an answer. Check the script only after the listening task, then correct any misunderstanding.

Listen More Closely

Separate what the account states from what you infer. Support an interpretation with a detail; consider another reasonable reading.

  1. Why might the member’s complaint be reasonable, even though clarification helped?
  2. What is the difference between clarifying information and agreeing on an action in this account?

Group Discussion

Use the story as a starting point for the wider questions below. Distinguish what the narrator describes from your own opinions and predictions.

Discuss the questions in a small group. Take turns and build on a point made by someone else.

  1. Why is it important to remove distractions before a conversation?
  2. What are two ways to show that you are listening carefully?
  3. When might an open-ended question be more useful than a yes/no question? Give an example.
  4. What might you miss if you plan your response while another person is still talking?
  5. What is one listening habit you would like to improve after hearing this story? Explain your answer.

Try It Again

Retell the main problem and response in your own words. Ask a partner one follow-up question, then make your retelling clearer.

Listening Script

The meeting that almost went wrong

Fictional story • read aloud by a teacher or partner

Our study club usually meets in Room Two at four in the afternoon. Last Monday, the organizer sent a short message: Meet in the new room at the usual time. I knew the time, but I did not know which room was new. Instead of guessing, I asked for the room number. The organizer replied that we should use Room Five because Room Two needed repairs. I repeated the plan back: Room Five, four o’clock, Monday. She confirmed it and sent a clearer message to the whole group. Before the meeting, I also asked who was bringing the books. It turned out that nobody had agreed to bring them, so I offered. We started on time, and everyone had something to read.

Afterward, one member said that asking so many questions had slowed everything down. I understood her point: nobody wanted a long exchange about a simple meeting. But the original message had left two different gaps. The room number needed a precise answer; the books needed someone to accept a responsibility. Simply agreeing that the plan sounded fine would not have settled either problem. Next time, I would ask the organizer to put the place, time, and responsibilities in one message. I would also check that everyone had seen the correction. A clear message is useful only if it reaches the people who need it.

For independent reading practice, read the story once, cover it, and answer from memory. This is reading recall; it does not replace listening to another person.
