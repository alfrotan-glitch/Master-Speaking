The Art of Goal Setting

Before Listening

Work with a teacher or partner. Keep the script at the end of this chapter covered until after the second listening.

Listening Strategy

First Listening

Listen to the whole story without reading the script. What is the main problem or decision? Give a one-sentence answer.

Second Listening

Read the detail questions, listen again, and take short notes in a notebook under the two headings below. Ask to hear a part again if you need it.

Main PointDetails I Heard

Listening Comprehension

Answer in short phrases. Use details you heard, not guesses about what usually happens.

  1. What was the original daily target?
  2. What did the speaker actually want to do?
  3. On which three days would the new practice happen?
  4. How long would each practice take?
  5. When would the speaker review the plan?

Compare and Clarify

Compare answers with a partner. Explain which detail supports an answer. Check the script only after the listening task, then correct any misunderstanding.

Listen More Closely

Separate what the account states from what you infer. Support an interpretation with a detail; consider another reasonable reading.

  1. Why does the speaker reject word count as the only measure of progress?
  2. What can the two attempts show, and what can they not establish?

Group Discussion

Use the story as a starting point for the wider questions below. Distinguish what the narrator describes from your own opinions and predictions.

Discuss the questions in groups of three or four. Give everyone a turn and ask for clarification when a point is unclear.

  1. Do you think setting goals is enough to become successful? Why or why not?
  2. Have you ever worked tirelessly to achieve something? What was the result?
  3. What usually prevents people from reaching their full potential?
  4. Do you agree that some people are held captive by fear or failure? Give an example.
  5. What is one goal you have this year? What will you do to go the extra mile and achieve it?
  6. Is it better to focus on the final result or on your daily habits? Explain your opinion.
  7. Can trying too hard sometimes cause harm to your health or personal life? Why?
  8. What is your biggest objective in learning English? How will you achieve it?
  9. Think of a successful person you admire. Which expressions from today’s lesson describe that person best?

Challenge Question

Consider this view: a goal should guide your practice without ruling out other possibilities.

  1. Do you agree or disagree? Explain your opinion with reasons and examples.

Try It Again

Retell the main problem and response in your own words. Ask a partner one follow-up question, then make your retelling clearer.

Listening Script

A smaller plan that lasted

Fictional story • read aloud by a teacher or partner

At the beginning of the month, I planned to learn twenty new English words every evening. On the first day I did it, but by Thursday I was tired and had stopped. My friend asked what I actually wanted to do with the words. I said I wanted to take part in class discussions. We made a smaller plan: on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, I would choose two useful expressions and use them in a short spoken response. Each practice would take ten minutes. After two weeks, I would check whether I could explain a point without writing every sentence first. I missed one evening, but I returned to the plan on Friday. I had learned fewer new words than I originally intended, yet I was practising the skill I wanted to improve.

At our review, my friend asked whether I had lowered my ambition. I said the ambition was still to join a discussion, not to finish a word list. We compared two attempts on the same topic. In the first, I used more new words but could not explain my reason. In the second, I used simpler language and answered a follow-up question. That was useful evidence, although it did not prove that every part of my English had improved. For the next fortnight, I kept the short routine and added one conversation with a willing partner. If that was difficult to arrange, I would practise explaining the same idea from a different point of view.

For independent reading practice, read the story once, cover it, and answer from memory. This is reading recall; it does not replace listening to another person.
