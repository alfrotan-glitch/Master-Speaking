The Power of Emotional Intelligence

A person may solve a difficult problem yet struggle to listen when a colleague disagrees. Someone else may understand a lesson but find it hard to respond to criticism. IQ tests assess some cognitive abilities; they do not describe every skill used in daily life. Emotional intelligence offers a way to discuss how people notice emotions and respond to them.

Emotional intelligence involves recognizing and understanding emotions in yourself and others, then choosing how to respond. Managing an emotion does not mean pretending it is absent. You might notice frustration, pause before answering, and ask a question rather than react impulsively. The aim is a thoughtful, constructive response, especially when a conversation is difficult.

One model, popularized by psychologist Daniel Goleman, describes five components: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, and social skills. Self-awareness means noticing your feelings and their influence on your behavior. Self-regulation concerns your response to those feelings. Motivation helps you continue toward a goal. Empathy involves considering another person’s feelings and perspective; social skills help you communicate and work with others. This is one framework, not the only way to describe emotional life.

Consider a disagreement at work. A manager can dismiss an employee’s concern or ask what is making a task difficult. An employee can interrupt in frustration or explain the problem and suggest a next step. Listening, acknowledging concerns, and discussing disagreements respectfully can support trust and teamwork. These behaviors do not remove every workplace problem, but they offer practical choices during a conversation.

The same ideas apply in a classroom. Feedback may feel uncomfortable even when it is useful. A student can ask for a specific example, explain what is unclear, and decide what to try next. Working with others also involves taking turns and considering different perspectives. Emotional skills can support learning alongside subject knowledge; they are not a substitute for it.

You can practise these skills in small ways. Notice an emotion before naming someone else’s intention. Listen actively, ask for feedback, and pause before responding. Afterwards, consider what helped and what you might change. Understanding another perspective does not require agreeing with it, and managing your response does not mean accepting disrespect.

Emotional intelligence is useful because knowledge alone does not tell us how to handle every interaction. We also need ways to understand ourselves, communicate our concerns, and respond to other people. Treat the framework as an invitation to practise those skills, not as a label that divides people into good and bad personalities.
