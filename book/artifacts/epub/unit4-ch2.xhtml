Listening Strategy

First Listening

Listen to the whole story without reading the script. What is the main problem or decision? Give a one-sentence answer.

Second Listening

Read the detail questions, listen again, and take short notes in a notebook under the two headings below. Ask to hear a part again if you need it.

Main PointDetails I Heard

Listening Comprehension

Answer in short phrases. Use details you heard, not guesses about what usually happens.

  1. How many credits did the club have?
  2. What did the books cost?
  3. Which two paid options did the club choose?
  4. How many credits remained?
  5. What replaced the paid event?

Compare and Clarify

Compare answers with a partner. Explain which detail supports an answer. Check the script only after the listening task, then correct any misunderstanding.

Listen More Closely

Separate what the account states from what you infer. Support an interpretation with a detail; consider another reasonable reading.

  1. Why is the final choice not evidence that one kind of spending always brings more happiness?
  2. What concern does inviting new members to help plan the conversation address?

Group Discussion

Use the story as a starting point for the wider questions below. Distinguish what the narrator describes from your own opinions and predictions.

Discuss the questions in groups of three or four. Give everyone a turn and ask for clarification when a point is unclear.

  1. In the story, the group chooses books and better lighting. What might make this a useful choice? Would another choice be better?
  2. Have you ever spent money on someone else and felt happier afterward? Describe your experience.
  3. How might a sudden increase in income create pressure in someone’s relationships? Give an example.
  4. In your opinion, which brings more happiness: buying something for yourself or giving a meaningful gift to someone else? Explain.
  5. Besides giving money, what other pro-social actions could help a group or community? Give an example.
  6. Which matters more in the situation you discuss: how much someone spends or what the money makes possible? Give reasons and examples.
  7. Have you ever pooled your money with friends or classmates to buy something for another person? If not, would you like to? Why?
  8. Could pooling money solve a practical problem in a community you know? What might make it difficult?

Some people believe that earning more money should be their highest priority, while others believe that helping others leads to a happier life. Which view do you support? Explain.

Challenge Question

Imagine you receive $100 that you must spend today. You have two choices:

Spend all of it on yourself.

Spend all of it helping someone else.

  1. Which option would you choose, and why? Do you think your choice would make you happier? Support your answer with examples.

Try It Again

Retell the main problem and response in your own words. Ask a partner one follow-up question, then make your retelling clearer.

Listening Script

One hundred credits

Fictional story • read aloud by a teacher or partner

Our learning club had one hundred imaginary credits for a planning exercise. We could buy books for forty credits, improve the lighting for thirty-five, or hold an event for thirty. Buying all three would cost more than we had. Some members wanted the event because new students had not met one another. Others said the room was too dark for reading. We agreed to buy the books and improve the lighting, leaving twenty-five credits unspent. For the new students, we planned a free conversation afternoon instead of the paid event. Not everyone preferred that choice, but each person had a chance to explain their reasons. Before ending the meeting, we wrote down the total and agreed to ask members whether the changes helped them use the room.

At our next meeting, someone asked whether the larger group had simply outvoted the newcomers. That question changed our discussion. Saving credits was easy to count; feeling welcome was not. We asked two new members to help plan the conversation instead of assuming that a free event would meet their needs. We also agreed to ask whether the room had become easier to use after the changes. The decision was not proof that books create more happiness than celebrations. It was a choice about this group, this room, and a limited budget. Another group might reasonably choose differently, especially if its members already had a comfortable place to read.

For independent reading practice, read the story once, cover it, and answer from memory. This is reading recall; it does not replace listening to another person.
