Living with AI: Friend or Foe?

Artificial intelligence, or AI, already appears in ordinary activities. A tool may help translate a message, suggest a film, generate an image, or draft an email. People also use AI to look for information, including health information. Using a tool does not make its answer reliable: a confident response still needs to be checked, especially when a decision could affect someone’s health, rights, or finances.

AI can process large amounts of information, recognize patterns, and handle some repetitive tasks quickly. Its usefulness and accuracy depend on the task, the system, and the data. A tool that performs well in one setting may fail in another. Businesses, hospitals, schools, and governments therefore need more than a demonstration of speed; they need ways to check results and decide who is responsible for them.

There are also risks. Automation can change the work people do and the skills they need. Collecting data can create privacy concerns, particularly when people do not know how their information will be used. AI can produce errors, invent plausible details, or repeat bias in its data. Human judgment is essential, but checking must be informed and deliberate rather than a quick glance at the answer.

In education, AI may help explain a concept, suggest practice, or summarize a text. It can also make it easier to submit an answer without understanding it. A useful test is whether you can explain the result yourself, check it against the source, and notice what is missing. Treat AI as a possible assistant to learning, not a replacement for your own thinking or a teacher’s guidance.

The question is not simply whether AI is a friend or a foe. Ask what a particular tool is doing, what evidence supports its output, and who may be affected if it is wrong. Responsible use includes protecting private information, acknowledging uncertainty, and keeping people accountable for important decisions. Learning to work with AI includes knowing when not to rely on it.
