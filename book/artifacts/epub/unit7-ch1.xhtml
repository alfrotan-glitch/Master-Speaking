The World Is Getting Smaller

Imagine waking up in Kabul, reading a message from Australia, drinking coffee produced abroad, and joining an online class with people in other countries. Each activity connects a local day with places far away. Not everyone has the same access to these connections, but the example shows how ordinary choices can be part of international networks.

A familiar product or conversation can have a much wider history than we first notice.

Globalization is the growing movement of people, products, ideas, and information across borders. Technology, transport, international trade, and the internet help make those connections possible. The term describes several linked processes, not a single invention or a change that affects every place in the same way.

A business might design a product in one country, manufacture it in another, and sell it internationally. A student might take an online course from a distant university. A small business might reach overseas customers through social media. These examples show how distance can become less of a barrier to some activities, although cost, regulation, language, and access still matter.

International connections can widen access to information, education, products, and work. They can help businesses reach new markets and allow researchers to collaborate on shared problems. Those are opportunities rather than guarantees. Whether someone can use them depends on resources and circumstances, and a benefit in one place may come with a cost elsewhere.

Culture moves through these networks as well. Someone in Kabul may listen to Korean music, watch an American film, eat Italian food, or speak with a friend in Japan. Music, food, fashion, and language can travel and change as people adapt them. Cultural exchange can introduce new choices without making everyone’s experience identical.

There are reasons for concern. Local businesses may struggle to compete with large international companies. Moving production can create jobs in one place and remove them in another. Local traditions may receive less attention as global trends become more visible. Benefits are not shared equally, so a discussion should ask who gains, who loses, and whose view has not been heard.

The environment is part of the discussion too. International transport uses energy and can produce emissions. Producing and moving large quantities of goods can contribute to pollution and waste. The effects depend on how goods are made and transported; a product’s foreign origin alone does not tell us its full environmental impact.

Globalization is therefore neither entirely good nor entirely bad. A useful response weighs opportunities against costs and considers how local communities, workers, cultures, and the environment might be protected. That requires looking at particular connections rather than defending or rejecting every international exchange at once.

When people say that the world is getting smaller, they do not mean that physical distance has disappeared. They mean that communication and interaction across distance can be easier. Globalization already affects daily life, but its effects differ. Ask how a connection works and what it changes for the people involved.

The question is not only how connected the world has become.

It is what kind of connections we want to build—and how their benefits and costs should be shared.

Speaking through Reading

Activity 1: One-Minute Summary

Work in pairs.

Student A: Give a one-minute summary in your own words. Include the main idea and a supporting example.

Student B: Listen for the main point, then ask two follow-up questions. Switch roles and use one response to improve your summary.

Activity 2

Discuss the questions. Support your answers with reasons or examples, and make room for a different view.

  1. What is the biggest advantage of globalization?
  2. What is the biggest disadvantage?
  3. Does globalization create more opportunities for young people?
  4. Can globalization help developing countries? Why or why not?
  5. Should countries protect local businesses from international competition?

Activity 3: Critical Thinking

  1. Can globalization make different cultures more similar?
  2. Is cultural change necessarily a bad thing?
  3. What aspects of your culture should never disappear?
  4. Can someone be a global citizen while maintaining a strong local identity?

Challenge Question

  1. If globalization eventually made most countries culturally similar, would that be a positive or negative development?
