Topic Language

Use this language when discussing the story and the wider topic. You are not expected to hear every expression in the story.

Vocabulary / ExpressionMeaningExample

Listening Strategy

First Listening

Listen to the whole story without reading the script. What is the main problem or decision? Give a one-sentence answer.

Second Listening

Read the detail questions, listen again, and take short notes in a notebook under the two headings below. Ask to hear a part again if you need it.

Main PointDetails I Heard

Listening Comprehension

Answer in short phrases. Use details you heard, not guesses about what usually happens.

  1. Where would the welcome event take place?
  2. What did one member first think break the ice might mean?
  3. What would partners share after introducing themselves?
  4. What time and day were confirmed?
  5. How did the organizer check that the first activity was clear?

Compare and Clarify

Compare answers with a partner. Explain which detail supports an answer. Check the script only after the listening task, then correct any misunderstanding.

Listen More Closely

Separate what the account states from what you infer. Support an interpretation with a detail; consider another reasonable reading.

  1. Why was knowing the individual words insufficient in the misunderstanding?
  2. What distinction does the ending make between shared communication and uniformity?

Group Discussion

Use the story as a starting point for the wider questions below. Distinguish what the narrator describes from your own opinions and predictions.

Discuss the questions in groups of three or four. Give everyone a turn and ask for clarification when a point is unclear.

  1. What knowledge exists in Afghanistan that is mainly passed down through local languages?
  2. What could be lost if a local language disappears?
  3. Do Afghan students sometimes feel that English ability = intelligence?
  4. Can someone be an excellent doctor, engineer, teacher, farmer, or businessperson without speaking excellent English?
  5. Has English opened opportunities for young Afghans? How?
  6. Can English also create inequality between people who have access to English education and those who don’t?

Mini Debate

“English proficiency should NOT be a requirement for university admission.”

Split the class into two groups.

Group A — Agree

Prepare three arguments supporting the statement, with a reason or example for each.

Group B — Disagree

Prepare three arguments against the statement, with a reason or example for each.

In both groups, use at least two of the following expressions in your arguments:

linguistic ability

gatekeeper

self-fulfilling prophecy

prohibitive — so costly that access becomes difficult

far-reaching consequences — effects felt in many areas or over a long time

Try It Again

Retell the main problem and response in your own words. Ask a partner one follow-up question, then make your retelling clearer.

Listening Script

Making the welcome clear

Fictional story • read aloud by a teacher or partner

Our international student group was planning a welcome event at the library. The organizer said we would break the ice at ten on Saturday morning. One new member asked whether that meant an outdoor activity. The organizer thanked her for asking and explained that break the ice meant helping people feel comfortable talking. The first activity would be indoors: introduce yourself to a partner and share one interest. A second person asked whether ten meant morning or evening, so the organizer wrote ten in the morning on the agenda. Before moving on, she asked two volunteers to explain the first activity in their own words. Their explanations showed that the instructions were now clear. The group kept the friendly expression, but added plain instructions that everyone could use.

Later, a member worried that explaining every expression would make our conversation dull. Another pointed out that she had understood every individual word but had still imagined the wrong activity. We did not decide to remove all idioms or make everyone sound alike. We decided to explain expressions when the practical meaning mattered, and to make it easy to ask without embarrassment. At the welcome event, partners could introduce a useful phrase from another language as well as explain something about themselves. English helped people take part in one conversation; it did not tell us how much they knew in other languages. A successful exchange would leave room for more than one way of speaking.

For independent reading practice, read the story once, cover it, and answer from memory. This is reading recall; it does not replace listening to another person.
