# Master Speaking

## Student Practice Pack

Practice for the self-contained course • October 2026

Use this pack with the book and Class Companion. It adds chapter success checklists, short speaking models, and worksheets for the eight original read-aloud listening stories used in Chapter 2 of each unit.

The models and listening stories are newly authored fictional teaching examples. A model shows one possible way to communicate; it is not a script to memorize or the only acceptable opinion. These short responses do not meet every full-length presentation requirement in the book.

Start by trying a task in your own words. Study the model, notice one useful move, then try again with a different example. Use keywords rather than copying whole sentences.

For each checklist, mark N = not yet, S = with support, or I = independently. Add a specific example of what you said or understood. These are local practice goals, not certified proficiency descriptors. Personal disclosure and recording are optional.

### Short model exchange — notice the moves, not just the words.

Mina: I think good listeners should ask more questions.

Omar: What kind of questions do you mean?

Mina: Questions that check meaning. Yesterday I misunderstood where our group was meeting.

Omar: So a question could clear up a misunderstanding?

Mina: Exactly. We also need to take turns, rather than interrupt.

Omar: I agree. Could asking too many questions interrupt the speaker as well?

Notice: Omar does more than agree: he checks Mina’s meaning and then asks about a possible limit to her idea.

### Short model response — an example to analyze, not a full timed assignment.

For me, listening is the most important communication skill. First, taking turns gives everyone a chance to make their point. Second, careful listening helps us clear up a misunderstanding before it becomes an argument. Last week, a classmate thought I had rejected his idea. I asked him to explain, then repeated what I had understood. We realized we were talking about different parts of the task. That taught me not to jump to conclusions. Listening also helps me support my opinion: I can answer the point someone actually made, rather than the point I expected to hear.

### Language support

- My main point is…

- One reason is… A second reason is…

- Could you explain what you mean by…?

Your turn: Discuss the most useful skill for a new club member. Give your own example and ask your partner one question about theirs.

### Short model exchange — notice the moves, not just the words.

Farid: I want to become a confident speaker this month.

Leila: What would that look like in practice?

Farid: I’d like to explain an idea for two minutes without reading a script.

Leila: How much time can you set aside?

Farid: Ten minutes on three evenings. I can record a short attempt, or just practise aloud.

Leila: That sounds manageable. How will you check whether your message is clearer?

Notice: Leila asks what the goal means and what time is available before suggesting a solution.

### Short model response — an example to analyze, not a full timed assignment.

My goal is to explain an idea for two minutes using only keyword notes. I can set aside ten minutes on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. First, I’ll choose a familiar topic and make a short plan. Then I’ll speak, note where I got stuck, and try again. After two weeks, I’ll ask a willing partner to summarize my main point. If they cannot follow it, I’ll work on my organization rather than simply trying to speak faster. When an evening is busy, my fallback is a one-minute attempt, not giving up for the whole week.

### Short model exchange — notice the moves, not just the words.

Nadia: My partner changed our slides without asking me. I felt ignored.

Samir: Was the main problem the changes, or not being asked?

Nadia: Not being asked. Some of the changes were helpful.

Samir: Have you told your partner that?

Nadia: Not yet. I could say I appreciate the work, but I want us to discuss changes first.

Samir: That explains your concern without assuming why your partner did it.

Notice: Samir checks which part of the situation matters instead of immediately taking sides.

### Short model response — an example to analyze, not a full timed assignment.

I think pausing before responding can help when a conversation becomes tense. It gives me time to separate what happened from what I assume it means. If a classmate changes my work, I might assume they do not respect me. But they may think they are helping. I would explain the effect on me and ask what they intended. That does not mean I must accept every change. It means I can discuss a boundary without beginning with an accusation. I can then suggest that we agree on changes together next time.

### Short model exchange — notice the moves, not just the words.

Amina: I’d spend the club’s money on a social event.

Bilal: I can see the benefit. My priority would be better lighting.

Amina: Why lighting rather than an event?

Bilal: People use the room every week, and some cannot read comfortably there.

Amina: Could we improve the room now and plan a free event?

Bilal: That might work. We should still ask the other members what they need.

Notice: The speakers discuss the effect of each choice and look for a compromise rather than judging each other’s priorities.

### Short model response — an example to analyze, not a full timed assignment.

If our learning club had a small budget, I would first ask what stops members from taking part. A celebration might be enjoyable, but better lighting could help people use the room every week. I would compare those benefits before deciding. The drawback is that practical improvements may not meet the need for friendship and relaxation. We could improve the room and arrange a free activity together. That is not the right choice for every club; it depends on what members need. My main point is that the price of an activity does not tell us its value.

### Short model exchange — notice the moves, not just the words.

Zara: I used a tool to suggest an outline, but one of its examples looks wrong.

Hadi: What makes you doubt it?

Zara: It gives a precise number without saying where it came from.

Hadi: Could you find the original source?

Zara: Not yet. I’ll leave the number out and use an example I can explain.

Hadi: You can also say how the tool helped, so the class knows which work is yours.

Notice: The speakers describe the specific evidence problem; they do not assume that confidence or precision makes an answer reliable.

### Short model response — an example to analyze, not a full timed assignment.

I would allow AI tools for brainstorming, but not as a substitute for checking facts or doing our own speaking. A suggested outline can help me get started. The risk is that I might repeat a convincing claim without understanding it. My rule would be to check factual claims against identifiable sources and explain what assistance I used. I would not upload classmates’ private information. If a tool is unavailable, the task should still be possible with paper and a partner. That keeps access fair and makes the speaking practice, rather than the technology, the main purpose.

### Language support

- The tool may help with…, provided that…

- The claim needs checking because…

- I cannot verify that yet, so…

Your turn: Write two proposed AI-use rules for a fictional speaking class. Explain how each protects learning or privacy. No AI account is needed.

### Short model exchange — notice the moves, not just the words.

Lina: Do you think liking someone is enough to make a partnership work?

Yusuf: It helps, but shared expectations matter too. Think about running a club together.

Lina: One person might expect a meeting every week and the other once a month.

Yusuf: Exactly. Neither plan is automatically wrong, but the difference needs discussing.

Lina: So goodwill does not replace an agreement about responsibilities.

Yusuf: I agree, though an agreement also needs reviewing when circumstances change.

Notice: The speakers use a neutral club example to discuss a broader partnership question. Learners may choose this route instead of personal relationship discussion.

### Short model response — an example to analyze, not a full timed assignment.

Goodwill matters in a partnership, but I do not think it is enough on its own. People can care about one another and still expect different things. In a fictional reading club, one organizer might expect weekly meetings while the other can help only once a month. Without discussing that difference, each could feel let down. I would suggest agreeing on responsibilities and reviewing the plan after a few meetings. An agreement cannot prevent every problem, but it gives people something clear to discuss. For me, respect includes being honest about what we can and cannot promise.

### Short model exchange — notice the moves, not just the words.

Roya: An online market could help our craft sellers reach more customers.

Aziz: It could, but what about shipping costs?

Roya: That is a real concern. We could start with local collection.

Aziz: Then how would sellers without internet take part?

Roya: Perhaps the community center could help them list their work, with permission.

Aziz: I’d support a small trial if we check both the costs and who is being left out.

Notice: Each proposed benefit is tested against an access or cost question. The speakers do not treat a prediction as an established result.

### Short model response — an example to analyze, not a full timed assignment.

I would support a small trial of an online craft market rather than assuming it will benefit everyone. It might help sellers find customers, but shipping costs and internet access could limit participation. A local collection option would reduce one problem. A shared support session could help with another, provided sellers control what is posted. After a month, we could compare costs, orders, and participants’ feedback. We would also ask who could not take part. The trial might show that the idea needs changing. That would be useful evidence, not a reason to hide the difficulties.

- Before extending the trial, we should check…

### Short model exchange — notice the moves, not just the words.

Tariq: At the start, we’ll break the ice.

Elena: Could you explain that expression?

Tariq: Of course. We’ll do a short activity to help people feel comfortable talking.

Elena: Thanks. What will participants actually do?

Tariq: They’ll introduce themselves to a partner and share one interest.

Elena: That is clear. I can explain those steps to the new members.

### Short model response — an example to analyze, not a full timed assignment.

For a multilingual audience, I would choose clear words and explain unfamiliar expressions. That does not mean removing every interesting phrase. It means noticing when a phrase gets in the way. If I say we will break the ice, I should also explain what participants will do. I can pause after each instruction and invite questions. Instead of only asking whether everyone understands, I can ask a partner to explain the first step in their own words. If the meaning is unclear, I can repair my message. Communication is a shared responsibility, not a test of whose accent sounds most familiar.

- Let me put that another way.

- By that expression, I mean…

- Could you tell me what you’ll do first?
