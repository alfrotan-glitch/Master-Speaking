# Master Speaking
## Teacher Implementation Pack

Teacher resources for the self-contained course • October 2026

Read alongside the book and the separate Class Companion. This pack provides the eight original fictional listening stories used in the book, matching questions and keys, reading keys, and a four-lesson teaching plan. The listening tasks are designed for a teacher or partner to read aloud; no external video or recorded audio is required.

The Unit 1 pilot deliberately uses four 75-minute meetings, one per chapter. It is a slower diagnostic trial than the Class Companion’s two-meeting unit plan. Do not promise both schedules cover the same work in the same time; use observed timings to choose whether to extend the course or reduce task coverage.

All listening accounts and speaking models in this pack are newly authored fictional teaching examples. They are not transcripts, research findings, quotations from real students, or evidence of a successful classroom trial. No live pilot has been conducted. Student-facing questions and models are exported separately so teachers can withhold scripts and keys during listening practice.

## 1. Delivery, preparation, and assessment

Choose the matching unit in the Student Practice Pack. For Chapter 2, use the matching original story in this pack and its five detail questions. The same script appears at the end of the listening chapter for checking after the task. Keep that section covered until after the second listening unless an access arrangement requires earlier text support.

Before each class: preview the topic for suitability; read the script aloud once; check the question key; print only the student pages needed; prepare a timer and grouping plan; identify one success criterion to observe. An internet connection, AI account, slide deck, or recording device is not required.

Teacher-read listening protocol (approximately 15 minutes)

- 0–2 minutes: students read the gist question and predict the kind of information they may hear. Explain only essential vocabulary that would otherwise block understanding; do not preteach the answers.
- 2–4: read the whole script once at a clear, comfortable pace, roughly 110–140 words a minute. Do not exaggerate stress on answer words. Students note the main problem or decision.
- 4–5: pairs compare their gist answers. Do not distribute the script yet.
- 5–7: students preview the five detail questions. Read the same script a second time without changing its wording.
- 7–10: pairs compare details and mark uncertainty. Offer a targeted replay if needed and note that support when comparing performance.
- 10–13: check answers against the key; ask which words helped. Accept accurate paraphrases and do not penalize minor spelling unless it changes meaning.
- 13–15: release the script for noticing and a short oral retell. A script provided before listening changes this into supported reading/listening; record the adaptation rather than claiming the same assessment conditions.

For an optional formative detail score, award one point per question, maximum five. For a question requesting two details, award half a point for each correct detail. Use the same rule for the whole class and distinguish an inaccurate answer from a language-form error. Gist and retell are feedback opportunities, not part of this five-point score. This is not a standardized listening test.

Use the five-dimension rubric in Class Companion Section 14 for speaking. Observe interaction in a paired exchange, not only a presentation. Give one specific strength and one next step. Do not grade accent identity, beliefs, extroversion, personal disclosure, or compulsory eye contact. Plan consent-based, private handling of any recordings.

The short models show useful moves; they are not full-length answers to every book assignment. Unit 1’s sample response includes five discussion expressions, but its six-turn dialogue is only a starter: students still need ten toolbox expressions to meet the book’s full Vocabulary Activation instruction. Use a lower target as documented scaffolding, not as evidence that the original requirement was met.

## 2. Unit 1 pilot • Lesson 1: reading into speaking

Length: 75 minutes. Book: Unit 1, Chapter 1. Print the Unit 1 models/checklist page; keep the reading key for teacher use. Learners may choose fictional situations. This pilot is formative, not a validated treatment or a promise of measured improvement.

Success evidence: each learner gives a main point in their own words, supports it with two reading details, and asks a responsive question. Give the same preparation time to learners whose performances you plan to compare.

- 0–7: entry task. In pairs, each learner speaks for one minute about a change that would improve a study club; partners ask one question. Teacher samples a few exchanges without giving a class-wide proficiency label.
- 7–15: discuss the book’s Before You Read questions. Accept different opinions; ask for reasons rather than a correct belief.
- 15–27: preview essential vocabulary, read the passage, and locate its main point. Learners note keywords rather than copying a paragraph.
- 27–39: complete the Vocabulary Check and five multiple-choice items. Compare answers and locate supporting words using the source-linked key.
- 39–47: use selected Answer the Questions items to distinguish a relevant paraphrase from a copied sentence. Allow brief planning support where helpful.
- 47–59: run Activity 1 in pairs: Student A summarizes for one minute; B asks two follow-ups. Switch roles, then use remaining time to improve the least clear part.
- 59–68: teacher shares two anonymous language examples; pairs repeat a 45-second summary using one improvement. Do not correct every error while students are speaking.
- 68–75: learners mark the Chapter 1 checklist and write an example of what they could do, not just a confidence rating.

Monitor: note whether a learner gives the overall message or only lists details; whether a follow-up responds to what was said; and whether meaning remains clear during word searching. Home practice: a one-minute explanation to oneself or a willing listener, followed by one revision. Recording is optional.

## 3. Unit 1 pilot • Lesson 2: listening and clarification

Length: 75 minutes. Use the original story “The meeting that almost went wrong” and the matching questions in Unit 1, Chapter 2 or the Student Practice Pack. Keep the script and keys separate until after listening. Record the input and any access support actually used.

Success evidence: identify the central problem, recover key details, and clarify a new plan in an exchange. Students should not be assessed on information the input does not provide.

- 0–8: retrieve three expressions from Lesson 1; each student asks a clarification question about a fictional meeting plan.
- 8–23: run the 15-minute listening protocol from Section 1 using the Unit 1 story.
- 23–33: reveal the script and examine the checking sequence: unclear message, specific question, repeated plan, confirmation. Have learners point to evidence.
- 33–43: teach “When you say…, do you mean…?”, “Let me check…”, and “Which…?” Practise with short exchanges rather than a chorus alone.
- 43–58: role-play. A privately chooses a room, time, materials, and contact person for a new club meeting. B must find those details by asking questions. Switch roles with a different plan; then change one detail and check the revision.
- 58–67: pairs report one misunderstanding and how they repaired it. Teacher gives anonymous feedback and asks for a clearer second attempt.
- 67–75: mark the Chapter 2 checklist. Record the input route, replay support, and one next listening goal.

Access: offer a written instruction card, seated pair work, and additional planning time. If a transcript is required before listening as an access arrangement, provide it and document the different conditions. Never deny access in order to preserve a score comparison.

## 4. Unit 1 pilot • Lesson 3: discussion that develops

Length: 75 minutes. Book: Unit 1, Chapter 3. Print the model dialogue and response. The models are fictional examples, not scripts to memorize or opinions students must adopt.

Success evidence: give a supported position, acknowledge a partner’s point, and ask a question that develops the exchange. Assess the quality of a contribution, not only the number of turns.

- 0–8: silent planning and paired warm-up: which communication skill matters most in a study club, and why?
- 8–18: read the six-turn model. Mark a clarification, an example, an acknowledgement, and a challenge. Distinguish a responsive question from an unrelated prepared question.
- 18–28: inspect the Speaking Toolbox. Partners select useful expressions and create a short starter exchange. Build toward the book’s ten-expression requirement; do not force all ten unnaturally into the first attempt.
- 28–43: groups of four discuss selected Guided Discussion questions. Rotate facilitator and participation monitor after seven minutes. Allow learners to use fictional examples.
- 43–51: collect feedback: one useful way of asking for a reason and one example of a claim that needs support. Learners revise privately before trying again.
- 51–65: practise the book’s two-minute Speaking Challenge in pairs, with keywords only. Each student uses five toolbox expressions, two reasons, and a personal or fictional example. Switch speaking/listening roles; listeners ask one follow-up.
- 65–75: peer feedback, a short repeated attempt, and Chapter 3 checklist. Note whether expression use helped meaning or sounded forced.

Extension: ask a learner to acknowledge a counterargument before defending a position. Support: provide a reason/example frame and a shorter rehearsal before the full task. A shorter supported rehearsal is useful practice but is not completion of the full timed challenge.

## 5. Unit 1 pilot • Lesson 4: presentation and questions

Length: 75 minutes. Book: Unit 1, Chapter 4. Students prepare a three-to-four-minute presentation with three points, examples, a conclusion, and the book’s language requirements. One presentation topic can be “How can our study club communicate more clearly?” Present a fictional club if preferred.

For this pilot, presentations are practised simultaneously in groups of three. The teacher samples performances for formative feedback; this does not constitute formal individual assessment of every learner. Arrange separate assessment slots if formal scores are required.

- 0–8: retrieve opening, signposting, and closing expressions. Discuss how an opening helps a listener know what to expect.
- 8–18: model sentence stress using one sentence with different intended contrasts. Aim for intelligibility, not a single supposedly natural accent.
- 18–30: plan three points, with one example each. Identify which ideas come from the reading and which are opinions or fictional examples. If using another source, name it accurately; do not add an unsupported “research proves” phrase.
- 30–40: partner rehearsal of the opening and one point; give a specific clarity comment and revise.
- 40–61: in trios, each learner presents for three to four minutes, takes up to two minutes of questions, and receives one minute of feedback. Rotate presenter, questioner, and feedback listener. The 21-minute window accommodates three seven-minute turns.
- 61–69: repeat the most difficult 45 seconds with a changed opening or clearer signpost. Teacher shares one observed strength and one next step.
- 69–75: complete the Chapter 4 checklist and pilot feedback form. Learners choose one speaking goal for the next unit.

If a learner needs an alternative to eye contact, accept other signs of audience awareness: an accessible handout, clear signposting, turning toward the group if comfortable, or inviting questions. If the student has no visual aid, do not insist on using “As you can see…” merely to reach an expression count.

## 6. Unit 1 reading key • scope and use

This key covers Unit 1, Chapter 1 only: five vocabulary completions, five multiple-choice questions, and five open reading questions. No True/False exercise is present in that chapter. Closed-reading keys for Units 2–8 appear in Section 16. The original listening stories have their own detail keys in Sections 7–14; open questions allow supported alternatives.

Answers are grounded in the revised passage or vocabulary table, not independently validated claims about the real world. Each quoted evidence phrase is checked against the current semantic book during QA. Use question stems rather than page numbers, since pagination can change. Accept accurate paraphrases for the five open questions. Critical Thinking, personal connections, and discussions have no single correct opinion.

For Find the Evidence, learners may cite regular participation and feedback in Sara’s example, the comparison with other practised skills, and the closing invitation to learn from each conversation. This is a set of possible textual supports, not a demand to reproduce three fixed sentences.

For the personal-connection sentences, check that the proposed action is specific enough to try. For example, “ask one follow-up question in each partner discussion” is more observable than “be better at English.” Never require an actual private experience as proof of participation.

## 7. Unit 1 • Original listening story and key

The Power of Communication — The meeting that almost went wrong

Original fictional teaching script for the matching Chapter 2. Use the five detail questions in the main book or the identical Student Practice Pack worksheet. No external recording is required.

Delivery: read the following 226-word script twice using Section 1. Do not give students the script or key before the first listening unless an access arrangement requires it.

### Read-aloud script

Our study club usually meets in Room Two at four in the afternoon. Last Monday, the organizer sent a short message: Meet in the new room at the usual time. I knew the time, but I did not know which room was new. Instead of guessing, I asked for the room number. The organizer replied that we should use Room Five because Room Two needed repairs. I repeated the plan back: Room Five, four o’clock, Monday. She confirmed it and sent a clearer message to the whole group. Before the meeting, I also asked who was bringing the books. It turned out that nobody had agreed to bring them, so I offered. We started on time, and everyone had something to read.

Afterward, one member said that asking so many questions had slowed everything down. I understood her point: nobody wanted a long exchange about a simple meeting. But the original message had left two different gaps. The room number needed a precise answer; the books needed someone to accept a responsibility. Simply agreeing that the plan sounded fine would not have settled either problem. Next time, I would ask the organizer to put the place, time, and responsibilities in one message. I would also check that everyone had seen the correction. A clear message is useful only if it reaches the people who need it.

Gist — suggested answer: A club member clarifies an unclear plan and helps the meeting run smoothly.

### Detail key — one point each; accept accurate paraphrases.

1. What caused the uncertainty?

Answer: The message said new room without giving a room number.

Evidence: “I did not know which room was new.”

2. Which room did the club use?

Answer: Room Five.

Evidence: “we should use Room Five”

3. Why was the usual room unavailable?

Answer: It needed repairs.

Evidence: “Room Two needed repairs”

4. How did the speaker check the meeting plan?

Answer: By repeating the room, time, and day back and receiving confirmation.

Evidence: “I repeated the plan back: Room Five, four o’clock, Monday.”

5. What responsibility did the speaker take on?

Answer: Bringing the books.

Evidence: “nobody had agreed to bring them, so I offered”

### Interpretation — suggested reasoning, not a required opinion

Why might the member’s complaint be reasonable, even though clarification helped?

Suggested reasoning: Repeated questions can take time; the listener should explain which gaps actually need checking.

Supporting detail: “nobody wanted a long exchange about a simple meeting”

What is the difference between clarifying information and agreeing on an action in this account?

Suggested reasoning: Clarifying establishes the room and time; agreeing on action establishes who will bring the books.

Supporting detail: “the books needed someone to accept a responsibility”

Transfer: use the matching Your turn activity in the Student Practice Pack. Assess the new exchange, not whether a learner reproduces the script’s wording or opinion.

## 8. Unit 2 • Original listening story and key

The Art of Goal Setting — A smaller plan that lasted

Original fictional teaching script for the matching Chapter 2. Use the five detail questions in the main book or the identical Student Practice Pack worksheet. No external recording is required.

Delivery: read the following 248-word script twice using Section 1. Do not give students the script or key before the first listening unless an access arrangement requires it.

### Read-aloud script

At the beginning of the month, I planned to learn twenty new English words every evening. On the first day I did it, but by Thursday I was tired and had stopped. My friend asked what I actually wanted to do with the words. I said I wanted to take part in class discussions. We made a smaller plan: on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, I would choose two useful expressions and use them in a short spoken response. Each practice would take ten minutes. After two weeks, I would check whether I could explain a point without writing every sentence first. I missed one evening, but I returned to the plan on Friday. I had learned fewer new words than I originally intended, yet I was practising the skill I wanted to improve.

At our review, my friend asked whether I had lowered my ambition. I said the ambition was still to join a discussion, not to finish a word list. We compared two attempts on the same topic. In the first, I used more new words but could not explain my reason. In the second, I used simpler language and answered a follow-up question. That was useful evidence, although it did not prove that every part of my English had improved. For the next fortnight, I kept the short routine and added one conversation with a willing partner. If that was difficult to arrange, I would practise explaining the same idea from a different point of view.

Gist — suggested answer: A learner replaces an ambitious word target with a smaller plan connected to speaking.

### Detail key — one point each; accept accurate paraphrases.

1. What was the original daily target?

Answer: Twenty new English words every evening.

Evidence: “twenty new English words every evening”

2. What did the speaker actually want to do?

Answer: Take part in class discussions.

Evidence: “I wanted to take part in class discussions”

3. On which three days would the new practice happen?

Answer: Monday, Wednesday, and Friday.

Evidence: “on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday”

4. How long would each practice take?

Answer: Ten minutes.

Evidence: “Each practice would take ten minutes.”

5. When would the speaker review the plan?

Answer: After two weeks.

Evidence: “After two weeks, I would check”

### Interpretation — suggested reasoning, not a required opinion

Why does the speaker reject word count as the only measure of progress?

Suggested reasoning: The aim is to participate in discussion; explaining a reason and answering a question show something a word total does not.

Supporting detail: “the ambition was still to join a discussion, not to finish a word list”

What can the two attempts show, and what can they not establish?

Suggested reasoning: They illustrate a difference on one task, not improvement in every aspect of English.

Supporting detail: “it did not prove that every part of my English had improved”

## 9. Unit 3 • Original listening story and key

Emotional Intelligence — Before making an assumption

Original fictional teaching script for the matching Chapter 2. Use the five detail questions in the main book or the identical Student Practice Pack worksheet. No external recording is required.

Delivery: read the following 227-word script twice using Section 1. Do not give students the script or key before the first listening unless an access arrangement requires it.

### Read-aloud script

During our group project, Kareem spoke very little. At first, I thought he was not interested. I nearly told the teacher that he was not helping. Instead, I asked him privately whether the task was clear. He said he understood it, but the group moved so quickly that he could not find a moment to speak. He had already written several useful suggestions in his notebook. At the next meeting, we gave everyone a minute to explain one idea without interruption. Kareem suggested a simpler way to organize our presentation, and we used it. I learned that silence did not tell me enough about his effort or interest. Asking a question gave me information that my first assumption had missed.

The change did not solve every disagreement. Later, I wanted a dramatic opening, while Kareem preferred a quiet explanation. This time, I could feel myself becoming impatient. I asked for a minute to write down what each opening would help the audience understand. We then tried both versions with another group. Their questions helped us choose, although Kareem and I still preferred different styles. I had first treated his silence as evidence of his attitude. It was really a reason to ask a question. Listening to him did not mean agreeing with every suggestion; it meant giving his ideas a fair hearing before we made a decision.

Gist — suggested answer: A question reveals why a quiet group member has not been speaking.

### Detail key — one point each; accept accurate paraphrases.

1. What did the speaker first assume about Kareem?

Answer: That he was not interested.

Evidence: “I thought he was not interested”

2. Did Kareem understand the task?

Answer: Yes.

Evidence: “He said he understood it”

3. What made it difficult for him to contribute?

Answer: The group moved too quickly for him to find a turn.

Evidence: “the group moved so quickly that he could not find a moment to speak”

4. What change did the group make?

Answer: Everyone received a minute to explain an idea without interruption.

Evidence: “we gave everyone a minute to explain one idea without interruption”

5. What did Kareem contribute?

Answer: A simpler way to organize the presentation.

Evidence: “a simpler way to organize our presentation”

### Interpretation — suggested reasoning, not a required opinion

What was the weakness in the narrator’s first judgment about Kareem?

Suggested reasoning: The narrator inferred an attitude from silence without checking the reason.

Supporting detail: “I had first treated his silence as evidence of his attitude”

How does the later disagreement distinguish understanding from agreement?

Suggested reasoning: They listen and test both openings, but do not have to share the same preference.

Supporting detail: “Kareem and I still preferred different styles”

## 10. Unit 4 • Original listening story and key

Money and Happiness — One hundred credits

Original fictional teaching script for the matching Chapter 2. Use the five detail questions in the main book or the identical Student Practice Pack worksheet. No external recording is required.

Delivery: read the following 236-word script twice using Section 1. Do not give students the script or key before the first listening unless an access arrangement requires it.

### Read-aloud script

Our learning club had one hundred imaginary credits for a planning exercise. We could buy books for forty credits, improve the lighting for thirty-five, or hold an event for thirty. Buying all three would cost more than we had. Some members wanted the event because new students had not met one another. Others said the room was too dark for reading. We agreed to buy the books and improve the lighting, leaving twenty-five credits unspent. For the new students, we planned a free conversation afternoon instead of the paid event. Not everyone preferred that choice, but each person had a chance to explain their reasons. Before ending the meeting, we wrote down the total and agreed to ask members whether the changes helped them use the room.

At our next meeting, someone asked whether the larger group had simply outvoted the newcomers. That question changed our discussion. Saving credits was easy to count; feeling welcome was not. We asked two new members to help plan the conversation instead of assuming that a free event would meet their needs. We also agreed to ask whether the room had become easier to use after the changes. The decision was not proof that books create more happiness than celebrations. It was a choice about this group, this room, and a limited budget. Another group might reasonably choose differently, especially if its members already had a comfortable place to read.

Gist — suggested answer: Club members explain different priorities and agree on a budget compromise.

### Detail key — one point each; accept accurate paraphrases.

1. How many credits did the club have?

Answer: One hundred.

Evidence: “one hundred imaginary credits”

2. What did the books cost?

Answer: Forty credits.

Evidence: “buy books for forty credits”

3. Which two paid options did the club choose?

Answer: Books and improved lighting.

Evidence: “buy the books and improve the lighting”

4. How many credits remained?

Answer: Twenty-five.

Evidence: “leaving twenty-five credits unspent”

5. What replaced the paid event?

Answer: A free conversation afternoon.

Evidence: “a free conversation afternoon instead of the paid event”

Suggested reasoning: It responds to one group’s needs and budget; another group could have different priorities.

## 11. Unit 5 • Original listening story and key

Living with AI — A confident answer was not enough

Original fictional teaching script for the matching Chapter 2. Use the five detail questions in the main book or the identical Student Practice Pack worksheet. No external recording is required.

Delivery: read the following 239-word script twice using Section 1. Do not give students the script or key before the first listening unless an access arrangement requires it.

### Read-aloud script

I asked an AI tool to help me draft an announcement for our club meeting. It produced a clear paragraph and said we would meet on Thursday in Room Three. I had not given it those details. Before sharing the announcement, I checked the noticeboard. The actual meeting was on Wednesday in Room Five. I corrected the day and room, then asked the organizer to check the whole message. I kept the tool’s suggested order of information because it was useful, but I did not trust the invented details. I also left classmates’ phone numbers out of the tool. When I shared the final announcement, I explained that a tool had helped with the draft and that the organizer had confirmed the meeting information.

A classmate asked why I had used the tool at all if I needed to check its work. I showed her my rough notes and the clearer order in the draft. That help was real, but it was different from knowing when our meeting would happen. The confident tone had made the invented details easy to overlook. We agreed on a simple rule for our next announcement: keep a record of the facts we supply, mark anything the tool adds, and check those additions before sharing. If we could not verify a detail, we would leave it out or say that it was unconfirmed. A polished paragraph should make our responsibility clearer, not hide it.

Gist — suggested answer: A learner keeps useful drafting help but verifies invented details before sharing a message.

### Detail key — one point each; accept accurate paraphrases.

1. Which day did the tool invent?

Answer: Thursday.

Evidence: “meet on Thursday in Room Three”

2. What were the verified day and room?

Answer: Wednesday, Room Five.

Evidence: “on Wednesday in Room Five”

3. Where did the speaker first check the meeting details?

Answer: On the noticeboard.

Evidence: “I checked the noticeboard”

4. What part of the draft did the speaker keep?

Answer: The suggested order of information.

Evidence: “I kept the tool’s suggested order of information”

5. What private information was kept out of the tool?

Answer: Classmates’ phone numbers.

Evidence: “I also left classmates’ phone numbers out of the tool.”

### Interpretation — suggested reasoning, not a required opinion

What useful contribution did the tool make, and what did that contribution not establish?

Suggested reasoning: It improved the order of information; that did not make its added meeting details reliable.

Supporting detail: “That help was real, but it was different from knowing when our meeting would happen”

Why might an apparently polished answer require particular care?

Suggested reasoning: A confident presentation can conceal invented or unchecked details.

Supporting detail: “The confident tone had made the invented details easy to overlook”

## 12. Unit 6 • Original listening story and key

Is Love Enough? — Friends with different plans

Original fictional teaching script for the matching Chapter 2. Use the five detail questions in the main book or the identical Student Practice Pack worksheet. No external recording is required.

Delivery: read the following 235-word script twice using Section 1. Do not give students the script or key before the first listening unless an access arrangement requires it.

### Read-aloud script

Two friends, Mariam and Salim, offered to run a community reading club. They both liked the idea, but they had not discussed the work involved. Mariam expected to meet every week. Salim could help only once a month because of his work schedule. Their first conversation became tense because each thought the other had changed the plan. When they compared their notes, they realized they had never agreed on a schedule. They decided to hold one meeting a month for the first three months. Mariam would choose a short reading, and Salim would arrange the room. They would share the task of welcoming new members. They still needed to decide how to pay for printing, so they wrote that question down for their next conversation.

Mariam later said that accepting a monthly meeting had felt like caring less about the project. Salim had understood the same agreement differently: he thought it made a promise he could actually keep. Neither meaning had been obvious during their first conversation. They agreed to review the arrangement after three meetings and to explain early if a responsibility became difficult. They also left room to disagree about the project without treating every disagreement as a judgment about their friendship.
