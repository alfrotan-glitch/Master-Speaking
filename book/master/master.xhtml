Foreword

This book begins with a simple belief: learning English means more than knowing what to say. It means finding a way to express what you think.

As your English develops, you need more than the language of everyday exchanges. You need to explain an idea, question an assumption, tell a story, disagree respectfully, and give reasons for your opinions. You also need to listen when an answer is not simple.

That is what this book is designed to help you do.

The topics in this book connect English with the world around you: relationships, globalization, language, culture, and the choices that shape our future. Readings and listening activities introduce different perspectives. You will discuss them, learn useful expressions, investigate questions, and present ideas of your own.

Some of that work may take you beyond familiar conversations.

A discussion may challenge something you believe. A speaker may make a point you disagree with. You do not have to accept every argument you encounter; you do need to understand it before you respond.

Your English classroom should be a place where you can think out loud.

You may hesitate, make a mistake, or change your mind. You may hear a stronger argument or find yourself considering a view you had not taken seriously before.

Use those moments to ask a better question, revise an explanation, or try a different way of making your point.

Take an active part in your own progress. Prepare, but do not wait for someone to give you every word. Leave room to think while you speak. After a conversation, notice one thing that worked and one thing to try differently.

Give yourself opportunities to use English, listen to feedback, and try again.

The journey starts here.

Turn the page—and let’s begin.

How to Use This Book

A Practical Guide for Students

Welcome to Master Speaking, a course from The TOEFL House.

Use this book to practise explaining, questioning, discussing, and presenting ideas in English.

Each unit gives you several ways to explore a topic. You are not expected to produce a perfect answer. Prepare your ideas, speak in your own words, and use feedback to make the next attempt clearer.

Prepare a few key words, speak in your own words, listen for your partner’s main point, and ask a follow-up question. After feedback, repeat part of the task and notice one improvement. Aim for clear, thoughtful communication rather than memorized answers or imitation of a particular accent.

For personal or sensitive questions, you may use a fictional example or discuss a comparable team or community situation. You do not need to disclose private information. Respect other perspectives and ask permission before recording anyone.

When presenting research, identify the author or organization, the source title, and the date or page where available. Explain what the evidence supports, distinguish facts from opinions, and acknowledge uncertainty. Do not invent statistics or references.

The Power of Communication

Imagine a day without communication. You cannot ask for directions, explain an idea, tell someone how you feel, or understand what they need. Buying food, attending a class, making a friend, or working with colleagues would be difficult. Communication is not just a subject to study. It is part of how we take part in everyday life.

Communication involves more than speaking well. It includes listening carefully, asking meaningful questions, understanding body language, writing clearly, and responding appropriately. The purpose is not simply to finish what you want to say. It is to exchange meaning. That makes communication a two-way process: you need to express your own ideas and make room for someone else’s.

It is tempting to wait until your English feels perfect before joining a conversation. But waiting also means missing opportunities to practise. Start with the language you have. Notice when your listener understands you and when you need to explain something differently. Ask for feedback, then try again. A mistake gives you something specific to work on; it does not mean the conversation has failed.

Consider two students, Ali and Sara. Ali knows thousands of English words but rarely speaks because he is afraid of making mistakes. Sara knows fewer words, but she joins discussions, asks questions, and uses feedback to improve. In this example, Sara becomes a more confident speaker through regular practice. Ali improves more slowly because he avoids communication. The contrast is not about who is more intelligent. It is about how each student uses opportunities to speak.

Listening deserves the same attention as speaking. Good listeners follow the speaker’s ideas and consider their feelings and intentions. They can ask follow-up questions or briefly restate the main point to check their understanding. Eye contact and body language vary across people and cultures; attention does not have to look the same for everyone. What matters is making an effort to understand, rather than simply waiting for your turn.

Communication also helps people solve problems and share knowledge. Families use it to make decisions; colleagues use it to agree on action. Teachers explain lessons, doctors ask about patients’ needs, and scientists explain their research. Each situation calls for different language, but each depends on making meaning clear.

Like learning to drive, play football, or use a computer, learning to communicate takes practice. Try a clearer example, a useful question, or a revised explanation. Notice what helps your listener, and choose what to work on next.

When you hesitate to speak English, start with the point you want to make. Use the words you know, listen carefully, and ask for clarification when you need it. You can work on accuracy while taking part. Let each conversation guide what you practise next.

Dreams Become Reality through Goals

A dream gives you something to imagine: becoming a doctor, starting a business, travelling, or speaking another language with confidence. A goal begins to turn that possibility into a plan. It names something you want to do and gives you a reason to take the next step. Neither a dream nor a written goal can do the work for you.

The difference between a wish and a goal is action. Wishing to improve your English does not tell you what to practise today. A workable goal connects the result you want with smaller steps you can take. You need a direction, a routine, and a way to notice whether the routine is helping.

Consider two university students learning English. The first says, ‘I want to speak fluently someday,’ but rarely practises. The second plans to learn 20 words a week, read an article each day, and speak for 15 minutes after class. In this example, the second student makes more progress because the plan is specific and followed consistently. Those numbers are an example, not a schedule everyone must copy.

A goal can help you decide what to return to when you meet an obstacle. Exams, work, illness, or family responsibilities may interrupt a routine. Rather than treating the interruption as proof of failure, review your plan. What can you still do? What needs to change? Continuing may mean taking a smaller step, not simply trying harder.

That is why flexibility matters. A plan that worked last month may no longer fit your circumstances. You might change the time you practise, reduce a task, ask for help, or reconsider the goal itself. Review your progress regularly. Changing an unrealistic goal can be a thoughtful decision rather than a failure to keep a promise.

Writing a goal down makes it easier to review. Record what you intend to do, how you will practise, and when you will check the result. A supportive friend, teacher, or family member can ask how the plan is going. Sharing a goal may strengthen your commitment, but choose support that helps rather than adds pressure.

Notice small achievements as well as unfinished work. Perhaps you asked a question without reading a script, completed a difficult task, or used feedback in a second attempt. Naming that progress can help you decide what to repeat. A useful record includes both something that improved and something that still needs attention.

A clear goal gives direction; repeated action gives you something to learn from. The point is not to follow a plan perfectly or compare yourself with everyone else. It is to choose a worthwhile next step, try it, and use what happens to make the following step more realistic.

The Power of Emotional Intelligence

A person may solve a difficult problem yet struggle to listen when a colleague disagrees. Someone else may understand a lesson but find it hard to respond to criticism. IQ tests assess some cognitive abilities; they do not describe every skill used in daily life. Emotional intelligence offers a way to discuss how people notice emotions and respond to them.

Emotional intelligence involves recognizing and understanding emotions in yourself and others, then choosing how to respond. Managing an emotion does not mean pretending it is absent. You might notice frustration, pause before answering, and ask a question rather than react impulsively. The aim is a thoughtful, constructive response, especially when a conversation is difficult.

One model, popularized by psychologist Daniel Goleman, describes five components: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, and social skills. Self-awareness means noticing your feelings and their influence on your behavior. Self-regulation concerns your response to those feelings. Motivation helps you continue toward a goal. Empathy involves considering another person’s feelings and perspective; social skills help you communicate and work with others. This is one framework, not the only way to describe emotional life.

Consider a disagreement at work. A manager can dismiss an employee’s concern or ask what is making a task difficult. An employee can interrupt in frustration or explain the problem and suggest a next step. Listening, acknowledging concerns, and discussing disagreements respectfully can support trust and teamwork. These behaviors do not remove every workplace problem, but they offer practical choices during a conversation.

The same ideas apply in a classroom. Feedback may feel uncomfortable even when it is useful. A student can ask for a specific example, explain what is unclear, and decide what to try next. Working with others also involves taking turns and considering different perspectives. Emotional skills can support learning alongside subject knowledge; they are not a substitute for it.

You can practise these skills in small ways. Notice an emotion before naming someone else’s intention. Listen actively, ask for feedback, and pause before responding. Afterwards, consider what helped and what you might change. Understanding another perspective does not require agreeing with it, and managing your response does not mean accepting disrespect.

Emotional intelligence is useful because knowledge alone does not tell us how to handle every interaction. We also need ways to understand ourselves, communicate our concerns, and respond to other people. Treat the framework as an invitation to practise those skills, not as a label that divides people into good and bad personalities.

Can Money Buy Happiness?

Does more money mean a happier life? The question sounds simple until you ask what the money is needed for. Paying for food or housing is different from buying another luxury item. People also value different things: security, health, relationships, meaningful work, or time to themselves. To discuss money and happiness, we need to consider those differences rather than search for one answer that fits everyone.

Money helps people meet basic needs such as food, housing, healthcare, and education. Having enough to pay essential bills or handle an unexpected expense can reduce financial worry. That makes financial security important. A discussion of happiness should not dismiss the practical difference between being unable to meet a need and having the resources to meet it.

More income does not guarantee that every part of life will improve. People can become used to a higher standard of living and begin wanting more. This process is often called the hedonic treadmill: an improvement becomes familiar rather than remaining as exciting as it first seemed. It is a useful idea to discuss, not a fixed income threshold or a rule that describes everyone’s experience.

How money is used matters too. A meaningful experience—learning a skill, taking a trip, or sharing time with people you care about—may offer something different from an expensive possession. Helping someone else may also bring a sense of purpose. Experiences can become shared memories, but neither generosity nor travel guarantees happiness. Their value depends on the person, the circumstances, and what needs the spending serves.

Earning more can also involve a trade-off. Extra hours at work may increase income while leaving less time for rest, relationships, or other responsibilities. For someone whose basic needs are not secure, that choice may be difficult rather than freely made. Consider both the benefit of the income and the possible cost to well-being before judging another person’s decision.

Money is a tool, not a complete measure of a life. It can provide security, choices, and opportunities. Meaningful experiences, generosity, relationships, and personal growth are also worth considering when deciding how to use it. A balance that suits one person may not suit another, and circumstances can change what is possible.

Instead of asking only whether money buys happiness, ask what a particular use of money makes possible—and what it may leave out. Does it meet an important need? Support a valued relationship? Create pressure elsewhere? A thoughtful answer connects money with the life someone is trying to build, rather than treating wealth itself as the final goal.

Living with AI: Friend or Foe?

Artificial intelligence, or AI, already appears in ordinary activities. A tool may help translate a message, suggest a film, generate an image, or draft an email. People also use AI to look for information, including health information. Using a tool does not make its answer reliable: a confident response still needs to be checked, especially when a decision could affect someone’s health, rights, or finances.

AI can process large amounts of information, recognize patterns, and handle some repetitive tasks quickly. Its usefulness and accuracy depend on the task, the system, and the data. A tool that performs well in one setting may fail in another. Businesses, hospitals, schools, and governments therefore need more than a demonstration of speed; they need ways to check results and decide who is responsible for them.

There are also risks. Automation can change the work people do and the skills they need. Collecting data can create privacy concerns, particularly when people do not know how their information will be used. AI can produce errors, invent plausible details, or repeat bias in its data. Human judgment is essential, but checking must be informed and deliberate rather than a quick glance at the answer.

In education, AI may help explain a concept, suggest practice, or summarize a text. It can also make it easier to submit an answer without understanding it. A useful test is whether you can explain the result yourself, check it against the source, and notice what is missing. Treat AI as a possible assistant to learning, not a replacement for your own thinking or a teacher’s guidance.

The question is not simply whether AI is a friend or a foe. Ask what a particular tool is doing, what evidence supports its output, and who may be affected if it is wrong. Responsible use includes protecting private information, acknowledging uncertainty, and keeping people accountable for important decisions. Learning to work with AI includes knowing when not to rely on it.

Is Love Enough?

Love is often described as the foundation of a successful marriage. Two people may care deeply about each other and imagine a future together. Yet sharing a life involves more than strong feelings. The question ‘Is love enough?’ invites us to consider what else a respectful, lasting relationship may need. You can explore that question through fictional people rather than private experiences.

Partners make decisions, share responsibilities, and respond to financial pressures or changes in their lives. These situations can reveal differences that were not obvious at the beginning. Emotional attraction alone does not decide how responsibilities will be shared or how a disagreement will be handled. Those questions need conversation and choices from both people.

Communication matters because agreement cannot simply be assumed. Partners need ways to express concerns and listen to each other without threats or humiliation. Avoiding a problem may leave it unresolved; discussing it respectfully may make the differences clearer. Understanding a perspective is a starting point for a decision, not a promise that every difference can be solved.

Trust is another part of the relationship. It involves more than expecting a partner never to make a mistake. It concerns honesty, respect, and whether each person’s words and actions give the other reason to feel safe. Constant suspicion can make trust difficult, but trust should not be demanded as a reason to ignore harmful behavior.

Expectations can create pressure too. One person may expect a partner to understand every feeling without being told or to meet every emotional need. Another may imagine that a good relationship never includes disappointment. Naming and discussing expectations gives both people a chance to consider which are realistic and which place too much responsibility on the other.

Disagreements are an ordinary part of long-term relationships; destructive conflict is not something anyone must accept. Listening, taking responsibility, and allowing time to think can help a respectful discussion. Compromise does not mean accepting coercion, threats, or violence. A lesson about communication should never be read as an instruction to stay in an unsafe relationship.

Not every difficulty begins inside the relationship. Work demands, financial problems, family expectations, social pressures, and cultural differences may also affect it. These outside factors can change what support people need. Discussing them helps distinguish a practical pressure from an assumption about a partner’s intentions.

A relationship may change as people and circumstances change. The excitement of a new relationship can become more familiar, and even healthy couples may experience disappointment or emotional distance. Shared time, appreciation, support, and continuing conversations can help people maintain a sense of partnership. Effort needs to be mutual, not a duty assigned to only one person.

So, is love enough?

Not on its own.

Love may bring people together; trust, respect, communication, commitment, and mutual effort shape how they share their lives. These ideas are a starting point for discussion, not a formula for every marriage. A thoughtful answer considers both the relationship people want to build and the boundaries each person needs to maintain.

The World Is Getting Smaller

Imagine waking up in Kabul, reading a message from Australia, drinking coffee produced abroad, and joining an online class with people in other countries. Each activity connects a local day with places far away. Not everyone has the same access to these connections, but the example shows how ordinary choices can be part of international networks.

A familiar product or conversation can have a much wider history than we first notice.

Globalization is the growing movement of people, products, ideas, and information across borders. Technology, transport, international trade, and the internet help make those connections possible. The term describes several linked processes, not a single invention or a change that affects every place in the same way.

A business might design a product in one country, manufacture it in another, and sell it internationally. A student might take an online course from a distant university. A small business might reach overseas customers through social media. These examples show how distance can become less of a barrier to some activities, although cost, regulation, language, and access still matter.

International connections can widen access to information, education, products, and work. They can help businesses reach new markets and allow researchers to collaborate on shared problems. Those are opportunities rather than guarantees. Whether someone can use them depends on resources and circumstances, and a benefit in one place may come with a cost elsewhere.

Culture moves through these networks as well. Someone in Kabul may listen to Korean music, watch an American film, eat Italian food, or speak with a friend in Japan. Music, food, fashion, and language can travel and change as people adapt them. Cultural exchange can introduce new choices without making everyone’s experience identical.

There are reasons for concern. Local businesses may struggle to compete with large international companies. Moving production can create jobs in one place and remove them in another. Local traditions may receive less attention as global trends become more visible. Benefits are not shared equally, so a discussion should ask who gains, who loses, and whose view has not been heard.

The environment is part of the discussion too. International transport uses energy and can produce emissions. Producing and moving large quantities of goods can contribute to pollution and waste. The effects depend on how goods are made and transported; a product’s foreign origin alone does not tell us its full environmental impact.

Globalization is therefore neither entirely good nor entirely bad. A useful response weighs opportunities against costs and considers how local communities, workers, cultures, and the environment might be protected. That requires looking at particular connections rather than defending or rejecting every international exchange at once.

When people say that the world is getting smaller, they do not mean that physical distance has disappeared. They mean that communication and interaction across distance can be easier. Globalization already affects daily life, but its effects differ. Ask how a connection works and what it changes for the people involved.

The question is not only how connected the world has become.

It is what kind of connections we want to build—and how their benefits and costs should be shared.

English: From a Local Language to a Global Lingua Franca

English is used in international business, science, aviation, education, entertainment, and online communication. In many settings it serves as a lingua franca: a language people use to communicate when they do not share a first language. How did a language once associated mainly with England and parts of the British Isles become so widely used, and what does that mean for its users?

The expansion of the British Empire was an important part of the story. English travelled through colonial administration, trade, education, and other institutions. That history involved unequal power, not simply a language becoming popular because people preferred it. Its effects differed across places and continue to influence opportunities and attitudes toward English.

During the twentieth century, the economic, political, scientific, and cultural influence of the United States helped strengthen English internationally. Companies, films, universities, and research institutions contributed to its visibility and use. British colonial expansion and later American influence are different parts of a complicated history, not a complete explanation of every community’s language choices.

Technology created further opportunities to use English across borders. Researchers, businesses, and people online could communicate with distant audiences. A person in Afghanistan might read an article from Japan, watch a video from the United States, and exchange messages with someone in Germany using English. Technology accelerated these connections; it did not make access equal or remove the value of other languages.

The people using a language also help shape it.

English is not used only by people who learned it as a first language. Millions use it as an additional language and adapt it to local needs. English in India, Nigeria, and Singapore has developed in different contexts and has different characteristics. These varieties remind us that international use does not mean everyone speaks in exactly the same way.

That leads to a question about authority as well as identity:

Who owns English?

When people use English across different first languages, should decisions about its use belong only to Britain, the United States, Australia, or other traditionally English-speaking countries? Users elsewhere also communicate, create, teach, and develop the language. Their experiences matter in a discussion about what English is becoming.

In that sense, English may be described as belonging to everyone who uses it—not as property, but as a shared means of communication.

This does not mean that British, American, Australian, or other first-language varieties are disappearing. They remain influential, but they are not the whole language community. A speaker may choose a particular model for a purpose while still respecting other varieties and accents.

Access to English can bring advantages in education, work, and information. Unequal access to good language education can also become a barrier. The same language may open an opportunity for one person and make a task harder for someone else. Consider the cost of learning it, whose knowledge is recognized, and which opportunities require it.

Local languages matter too. If younger generations have fewer reasons or opportunities to use an ancestral language, it may lose speakers. The influence of English is one issue to consider alongside other social pressures. Supporting international communication should not mean treating a community’s languages as obstacles to be removed.

The global use of English brings both opportunities and responsibilities.

It can connect people who would otherwise struggle to communicate, while raising questions about access, power, and linguistic diversity. A useful discussion keeps those concerns together rather than assuming that one language can meet every need.

A shared language need not produce identical voices.

One possible future is a multilingual world in which people use English to connect across borders while continuing to use, teach, and value the languages that give their communities meaning.

Pronunciation Clinic

Skill: The Schwa /ə/ — Making Unstressed Vowels Natural

The schwa /ə/ is a useful sound to notice in unstressed syllables. Its use varies across words, accents, and speaking situations.

The schwa is a relaxed vowel found in many unstressed syllables. Practise it to make the contrast between stressed and unstressed syllables clearer, not simply to speak faster.

The Schwa in Common Words

Notice how the unstressed vowel becomes weak:

about → /əˈbaʊt/

around → /əˈraʊnd/

support → /səˈpɔːrt/

today → /təˈdeɪ/

ago → /əˈɡoʊ/

perhaps → /pərˈhæps/

The first vowel in about is not pronounced strongly like the “a” in cat. It is relaxed: about → ə-BOUT

The Schwa in Academic Vocabulary

Compare these words from today’s topic. Most contain a schwa; linguistic is a contrast example without one.

globalization → /ˌɡləʊbəlaɪˈzeɪʃən/

communication → /kəˌmjuːnɪˈkeɪʃən/

international → /ˌɪntəˈnæʃənəl/

education → /ˌedʒəˈkeɪʃən/

linguistic → /lɪŋˈɡwɪstɪk/

professional → /prəˈfeʃənəl/

cultural → /ˈkʌltʃərəl/

different → /ˈdɪfrənt/

Notice how unstressed vowels are often reduced while stressed vowels remain more prominent.

The Schwa in Function Words

The schwa is particularly common in weak forms:

to → /tə/

a → /ə/

the → /ðə/

for → /fə/

of → /əv/ or /ə/

can → /kən/

Compare:

I want to learn English.

Natural speech:

I want tə learn English.

And:

English is a global language.

Natural speech:

English is ə global language.

The purpose is not to speak unclearly. The purpose is to make the important words stand out naturally.
